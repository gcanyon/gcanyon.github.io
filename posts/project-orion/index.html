Project Orion

My hard science fiction novella, complete, in fifteen chapters.

This is the complete novella. The original lives in a Google Doc; if you have thoughts, I’d love for you to leave a comment there.

Departure

“We’re positioning the anchor, please prepare for lift in two minutes.” The autopilot’s voice filled the passenger compartment with calculated calmness.

“First time going to space?” The boy – no, not a boy, a young man – leaned across the aisle. Purple bangs, an inch too long for their own good, slid over mismatched eyes, and an animated butterfly tattoo flitted across his right cheek, circled his ear, and then settled on his jawline, idly flapping its wings that perfectly matched the color of his hair. He brushed the bangs back with a clearly practiced carelessness.

Anya nodded, a lie.

Two rows ahead, a small woman with straight blonde hair cut just above shoulder-length wrestled with a toddler and a harness, cursing in Portuguese. Anya spoke Spanish, not Portuguese, as made sense in Argentina, but it was obvious the woman was lecturing the child about safety. The toddler squealed in delight and struggled harder, until with a series of snaps of the harness buckles she locked him in place.

On the young man’s far side sat a girl – no, not a girl, a young woman – with dark hair that was braided tight, matching her posture. She watched Anya the way a shepherd watches a wolf in the distance, waiting for it to move along.

The remaining twenty-five seats were empty. Not that it mattered much: the drive field was so ridiculously efficient at twisting gravity it would take only a few hundred watts to lift the cradle, so the journey was effectively free.

“You’ve got your harness right,” the young man said in confident approval. “That’s more than most first-timers manage. Not that it matters much in a cradle.” He smiled a grin that came with instructions. “I’m Tom.”

Anya actually had the harness right because she’d lost count of how many times she’d gone up, and had lied to him because she had been lying to boys of all ages since she was twelve. She offered a small, neutral smile back, and looked past him at the large picture window that framed the windswept Río de la Plata beyond Dock Sud.

Tom leaned into her field of view. “Heading to the Moon? G-ball championships? My cousin made regionals last year–”

“No.” She could have let him ramble, let it go, but her voice ran ahead of her brain. “I’m being dropped at a runabout heading for the planet Egeria at Gliese 581.”

Tom’s eyes widened; the young woman’s eyes narrowed, then relaxed somewhat as she realized Anya would be gone in under an hour.

Anya regretted it instantly. Next time, lie twice!

“Wow,” Tom said. “That’s – what – twenty light-years? Six months shipboard?”

“No,” she said, again too quickly. “The ’tross is running a higher multiplier. A few weeks ship’s time.” Anya’s voice trailed up at the end of the sentence. Lie three times. Shut up, shut up! Love the silence.

Tom inhaled for a new volley, but the young woman cut him off without looking at him, eyes focused on Anya. “Leave her alone, Tom.” The words, directed at him but meant for her, were low and resonant, with a tone that would make dogs behave.

Anya met those eyes. “I’m fifteen.”

The woman’s posture didn’t change, but her eyes softened – slightly.

“Yes, Rosa,” Tom said quietly.

The autopilot jumped into the lull. “Anchor is in position. Lift is imminent. Please ensure you are secured.” Everyone already was. The autopilot said it anyway, because why wouldn’t it?

Still, Anya knew “securing” was pointless. Sadly, there would be no rumble, no shaking, no hard press into their seats. The cradle’s drive field would twist the Earth’s gravity back on itself, and the cradle and its contents would launch as smoothly as Wonka’s elevator.

And, almost as if her thought had caused it, Buenos Aires fell away at 10 g’s.

She wished it could be like the old days of Apollo and Soyuz: fire and thunder, as she had seen watching all the launch videos on repeat. To her eternal regret, rockets like that – real rockets – had ended long before her parents were born.

Ahead of the cradle, tethered to it by a short cable, the anchor repelled the cradle and its contents with gravity magnified by a factor of billions… trillions? Anya wasn’t sure. Whatever the multiplier, it was enough that their apparent weight didn’t noticeably change as Earth repelled them.

As the city shrank below them, Tom leaned across Rosa for the viewport. “God, I love this part,” he said. “Falling up.”

Rosa didn’t look at Tom. She was also focusing on the window, her body even stiffer than before, as the sky turned a darker blue, then black. “Your love is loud,” she said, without heat. “Let other people have theirs quiet.”

Tom flushed. “Right,” he said, his smile shrinking a bit.

The Earth displayed its curve. The Albatross was stationed roughly over California, hovering at 400 kilometers, so in less than a minute, when the cradle hit 100 kilometers, effectively clear of the atmosphere, the drive field angled sharply to push against a swath of the Earth South Southeast of them, accelerating the cradle even faster roughly North by Northwest. Anya thought of the time her mother had forced her to watch the Hitchcock movie with her and frowned – unless her mother made the trip to 581 highly unlikely! some forty-two years would pass for her mother before they saw each other again.

Her father had engineered this situation, so he owned the blame for it – but her mother had committed similar violations. Her parents could be such assholes to each other. They had been for at least ten years. She had memories back to when she was five, just before everything had gone completely to shit, but even then she had no memories of her parents ever being kind to each other, or even to anyone in general. Maybe she just didn’t remember a time when they had been kind, or maybe they never had been; she didn’t know which made her sadder.

The toddler two rows up, his mother distracted by the lift, managed to release the harness, giggling, and tore off up the aisle. As the mother unbuckled to chase him, the autopilot returned with the same dry, comforting voice. “We are approaching the Equator. Please remain secured until we dock in approximately five minutes.” The mother spared an angry glance in the general direction of the disembodied voice, then managed to snag a wrist, pull the boy back, and get him into the restraints. The toddler sulked by shedding a shoe and chucking it ahead three rows.

Tom cocked his head at Anya, trying for gallant. “You okay?”

She nodded. “I’ve seen worse,” she said, and let herself have the private joke of telling the truth about a lie.

Rosa turned her eyes to Anya and weighed the words, then filed them without comment and returned her visibly stressed attention back to the window.

A few minutes later, the Stellar Albatross came into view: a flat ship designed for significant acceleration with minimal strain on the hull. It was just over sixty meters in diameter but only ten meters tall – the exact opposite of the rockets in the videos Anya had watched, both factual and fictional. Several kilometers above it – no, not nearly that far, Anya noted with curiosity – was a plate almost seventy meters in diameter, but under a meter thick: the anchor. Anya knew a tether strung between them, but it was invisible at this distance. She also knew the cradle’s anchor was not nearly as far above it; the geometry of the gravitational gradient in the cradle was noticeably curved.

The cradle and the Albatross traded info on their gravity fields, did the necessary math, and there was a perfect overlap of intentions as the fields merged without so much as a slap or a thunk, the two ships hovering in sync above the Earth. A few seconds later the light above the door at the front of the aisle changed from orange to green.

“Docking complete,” the autopilot said, sounding vaguely pleased. “Passenger Anya Morris please make your way to the Stellar Albatross. Be sure to collect all your belongings, and watch the gravity gradient as you approach the door, it will be roughly five degrees off apparent vertical.”

Belts popped. Velcro complained. The toddler whooped and launched over the back of his seat, only to be snagged by the ankle like a fish, to cries of infant outrage.

Anya undid her harness and stood. “Vertical” was indeed off vertical, and it took her a moment to balance. She had no luggage; she never took luggage. It wasn’t her first time on the ship, although it would be her first time outside the solar system. She nodded at Tom and Rosa and made her way forward. As she passed the mother and child, he made one last attempt to undo his harness, but his mother was paying attention only to him, and he managed none of the buckles without her immediately re-closing them.

Anya could feel the increasing tilt as she walked forward. The door swung open as she approached, and a man she didn’t recognize stepped forward. She had checked: she normally flew on the Albatross II, and the crew for this trip was all-new since the last time she had been aboard the Albatross a year earlier. The man wore a medic’s tri-band and a steward’s smile. He appeared to be late twenty-something, with dark wavy hair and a handsome, in a professional way, face. Anya noted that he was leaning backward, away from her and the edge of the Albatross.

“Welcome aboard the Stellar Albatross,” he said. The tag on his chest said BEN CARTER in block font, but he immediately followed with, “I’m Ben Carter. Watch your step as you come aboard, the gravity is significantly tilted.” He looked at the floor past her. “No luggage?”

Anya shook her head. As she stepped forward, she couldn’t resist looking over her shoulder at Tom. She’d chosen shoulder-length dirty blonde hair today, with a slight curl to it, and she brushed it back over her ear with a smile at Tom, but of course she was checking for Rosa’s reaction, and got the look she was hoping for. She had no interest in Tom really, but Rosa deserved at least a little worry for how she had treated him on the lift up.

Then she crossed over into the Albatross’s gravity and nearly fell over backwards. She heard Rosa laugh out loud. Shit! Well, they’ll both be sixty by the time I get back, so what does it matter?

Aboard

Ben turned and walked down the steel-framed hallway. The walls on either side were covered with hangings depicting various pastoral scenes – Anya remembered when she was twelve and her father had dragged her all over Ares City chasing down particular vendors that claimed to have hand-crafted the hangings. Anya was still unclear on why that mattered, or why Martians making art about scenes on Earth mattered, but she had tagged along behind her father as he secured dozens of them to decorate the Albatross.

Ben talked over his shoulder as he walked. “I’m one of the crew hired by your father to get you to Egeria. I understand the Albatross hasn’t changed much since the last time you were aboard, but I’m your aide-de-camp for the trip, so if you need anything, I’m your guy. If you lose something, tell me and I’ll find it. If you insist on chasing after it, let me know ahead of time who to inform we lost you.”

Behind them there was a slight metallic ping as the cradle separated from the Albatross to head off to the Moon, or perhaps wherever the mother and son were going, if they were going first.

As they walked down the hallway, Anya noticed the smell: the cradle, sterilized between trips, had smelled like new plastic and wiped-down metal and just a bit like nervous people. The Albatross smelled like the mobile home that it was: metal mixed with paper and cloth, a hint of body odor, and lots of coffee and tea. The hangings smelled vaguely like sheep. At least, she guessed so – she didn’t know what sheep smelled like, but she imagined it was like the hangings, only more. Anya remembered the smell from a trip to Saturn the year before; the balance had shifted toward coffee since then.

Ben continued. “I’m also the chief medic on board. We’re doing a high acceleration trip, so I’ll give you a quick once-over before we head out. Nothing too serious, just listen to your heart and lungs, and a quick scan to make sure everything’s in the right place. I assume you’re feeling okay?”

Anya nodded, then realized he wasn’t looking back at her and said, “Yes, fine.” She saw his head nod. “Why the messed-up gravity?”

“I’ll let Kenji explain that.”

The Albatross had a configurable interior, and some things had changed, so despite having been on board many times she studied everything to see what was different. Anya had inherited her interest in historical space travel from her father, and she noted the liberally-distributed handholds they walked past. Dad still pretending the Albatross will ever see zero-g

She followed Ben down the narrow hallway until he turned right into a small room with MEDICAL on the door. He examined her as promised, running several medical devices over her torso in an impersonal but professional way, and pronounced her fit to lift. They left medical and he escorted her further into the ship.

He led her along a slight curve that angled toward the ship’s center. They climbed a set of stairs to the uppermost level of the ship and into a meeting/dining/entertaining room that could seat thirty comfortably. She noted as she walked in that the set of marks of her height over the years were still on the entry door frame, many of them clustered around when she was six, shortly after her parents had broken up and her father had taken her on a tour of the solar system over most of a year, practically holding her hostage away from her mother, who didn’t like space travel one bit.

The tables and other furniture were bolted to the floor and handholds were closely spaced around the room. The ceiling, four meters overhead, was covered with a permanent display, indistinguishable from a window, showing nearly the exact view a window in that location would have. Against the backdrop of stars was the anchor mass: not glossy, not dramatic, its dark texture dully reflecting the Earthlight from below. The tether ran to it and vanished against it like a seam you’d only notice if you’d sewn it yourself.

“That’s the anchor,” Ben said unnecessarily. “Sorry it’s blocking the view. It looks like a mistake, like someone forgot to draw the stars in that section of the sky. But when you learn what it does for us, you might start saying ‘thank you’ when you pass a viewport.”

“What’s it doing for us?” Anya asked, because it was the right line to feed a man who liked knowing and sharing the answer.

“Keeping your world in place,” Ben said. “The gravity drive sets up repulsion between that plate and the ship, all the time. The tether keeps them from parting ways, and we get a constant proper acceleration out of it. One G of ‘down’ for the ship. I can remove it from the display if you like?”

“No, it’s fine,” Anya replied absently. “The ‘Tross has been re-rigged for higher acceleration for this trip, right?” Ben nodded. “Is that why the gravity is torqued?” Ben’s eyebrows raised a bit.

Shit, don’t be smart!

Ben recovered and replied, “Kenji will be delighted to hear you asked. He’s already spent so much time explaining to each of the rest of us, any of us could brief you in depth, but then he wouldn’t get to do it, and he’d be frustrated. It’s just a short detour to starboard, let’s get it out of the way. He’d tell you anyway, so you might as well ask and make him double happy”

He led her out of the meeting room, through a hall, down two sets of stairs, and down another hall to the edge of the ship. They stopped at a hatch with ENGINEERING stenciled on it and a series of stick-on warning triangles mixed with Pokemon and an animated image of Goofy scratching his head in confusion with a wrench in one hand, staring at a leaking pipe. As they walked up Goofy shrugged and banged on the pipe with the wrench, releasing a torrent of water. The hatch swung open on a whisper. Inside was a space about four meters across and six deep that was both chaotic and organized: filled with tools and machine equipment, parts in boxes lining the walls, and various cables and wiring harnesses hanging from the ceiling.

Kenji Tanaka looked up from a diagnostic panel as they entered. His black hair looked like it had dried in zero gravity, and he had the slouched posture of a man who’d fought a chair for a draw. He set down an impossibly small tool, pushed up a specialized set of display glasses and said, “Hi, I’m so glad you’re here. Anya? I’m Kenji.” He talked fast.

Ben nodded at Anya. “Kenji, she asked about the tilted gravity and the increased boost for the trip. I told her she was a fool to bring it up, but she was persistent, so go ahead and claim another victim.”

Kenji clapped his hands. “We’ve got five minutes before the Captain wants you on the bridge, which is eight longer than we need and… many fewer than I want.”

“Five being eight longer than we need implies negative time,” Ben interjected.

Kenji waved a dismissive hand at Ben, who smiled, shook his head and left. When Ben had gone, Kenji turned to Anya. “So, how much do you know about gravity drives?”

“Pretend I know nothing.” Not technically a lie.

“Okay. First, there are three gravity manipulators on board.” He held up three fingers to illustrate, gesturing to each in turn.

Main, gravity, deflector.

“The ship’s drive operates, surprise, surprise, on the whole ship: us, the ship, the anchor mass, all of it. It attracts or repels the ship from whatever we point it at. For this trip that will be the Earth, briefly, then the Sun, and when we arrive at 581 it will point forward at the star to decelerate us.”

Okay, ship, gravity, and deflector. Now tell me about the gravity.

Anya nodded appreciatively.

“The gravity drive you already know repels the ship from the anchor mass to give us onboard gravity.”

But why is the gravity wonky?

“The deflector is the most powerful drive on the ship. It projects the gravity of the entire ship and anchor forward at about 10^24 to one to push interstellar gas and dust out of our way and step down the wavelength of harmful radiation.”

“And the gravity?”

“I’m getting there.” He started talking even faster. “The gravity drive is much weaker than that, but still good to almost a trillion to one, which is way more than the ship’s drive at around a billion to one. Normally for a trip like this we’d hit about 99.97% c, and get to 581 in about six months ship’s time. That’s how your father travelled. Or I should say ‘is traveling’ – he’s not there yet, since he only took off two days ago on the Albatross II.

“The Albatross isn’t prepared for a six-month journey, so the trick I applied here is temporarily swapping the ship drive and the gravity drive.”

Anya’s eyes widened. Mad scientist much? “Doesn’t that mean we’ll have less than one gravity on the ship?”

Kenji nodded quickly. “Gold star for you, except not quite. The anchor is typically about three clicks away. I reeled it in to 95 meters so the effect will be strong enough even based on the weaker drive.” So that explains why the anchor looked so close. “It should be right around one G, but it could be a little variable, since the system isn’t designed to be used like this.”

“And that proximity causes the tilting: the point mass equivalent is at too much of an angle.”

Kenji’s eyes widened a bit. “Exactly! You have the mind of an engineer!”

Learn to be quiet, please!

Kenji picked up a small metal ball from a drawer in his work station. “Anywhere but the center of the ship, vertical is going to be noticeably off.” He set the ball down on the nominally flat surface next to him and it obligingly rolled off and into the back of the room toward the edge of the ship. “But there’s just the five of us for this trip, so staying near the center won’t be much of a challenge, except for fixed locations like engineering here at the edge of the ship. There’s also going to be a gravitational gradient, even across the height of a human. It’ll only be a few percent, and no harm, but you’ll notice it. Along with the boosts we’ll go through as we take off, that and the variability is the reason for the extra medical checks, just to be safe. Also, gravity will be noticeably less on the lower decks, as low as about 8 meters per second squared, but that’s mostly storage anyway, and that’s manageable when we have to go down there.

“Fortunately, your father, because he’s quirky…” Anya’s face darkened and Kenji twitched. “No offense, I like quirky! He had the Albatross designed for weightlessness, so even if we – sorry, I! – make mistakes, we’ll be fine, as long as I err on the side of too-low gravity mistakes rather than too-high.” He smiled at his very-much-not-funny joke. “We’ll hit 99.9991% c, and the twenty and a half light years to 581 will take us just about 32 days ship’s time.”

“Why not swap the repulsion drive in?” Please have a reason!

Kenji’s eyes widened. “I thought about it for a hot second, but there are several reasons not to. First, it’s too powerful and I couldn’t step it down enough to be practical. I could maybe fade it to 10^20 to 1, and the tidal forces would still crush us.”

Okay, that’s new. “Wait, what? Like spaghettification on your way into a black hole?”

Kenji nodded, but before he could reply, the communicator attached to the neck of his shirt said, “Mr. Tanaka, send our guest forward before she thinks Engineering is the whole ship.”

“Captain,” Kenji replied respectfully, “Engineering is the whole ship.”

“Then I will borrow a corner of it,” the voice answered. “Bridge. Now, please.”

Kenji stood and motioned for Anya to follow him, only barely hesitating in his speech as they walked. “Yeah. Falling feet-first into a black hole you get stretched because your feet are closer and get pulled harder than your head, until it literally rips you apart.” Despite how fast he talked, he positively strolled down the hallway. “Pushing off Earth or the Sun, we have the opposite problem. We get compressed because the repulsion from below is stronger the closer you are to the source. Too much multiplier, too close to the Earth or Sun, and we’d be crushed. Call it pizzafication.” Kenji turned left down a hallway Anya was certain wasn’t the shortest route to the bridge. The pace of his speech increased. “That’s why we’ll all be lying down when we leave Earth. That said, the real limiting factor is the Albatross itself and the anchor mass. I’ve done the math so everything mostly balances out, but it’s an unusual rig, to be sure.”

“Why not just build the Albatross to be faster?”

“There are a thousand design features that follow on from the gravity multiplier of a ship’s drive. You can’t just slap on a more powerful drive and call it done.”

“But isn’t that exactly what you’ve done?”

Kenji blinked, one foot on the bottom step of a set of stairs, then blinked again. “Well… yeah, but ships are designed with significant safety margins, and an extra 1000x gravity is well within those limits as long as we watch what we push near Earth. We wouldn’t be able to get the Albatross certified for a 1000x drive permanently, but for a one-off trip like this it’s not a problem. People do it all the time.”

“All the time?”

“Okay, people do it.” Kenji shrugged.

Anya hated saying the phrase, and avoided it whenever she could, but it really seemed appropriate here. “Does my father know you’ve done this?”

Kenji stopped walking, and Anya wished she hadn’t asked, both because she wasn’t going to like the answer, and because of what Kenji now thought of her. “He’s been briefed. The brief was high level. We can cancel the flight if you wish, or simply take the long way, but the Albatross isn’t prepared for a five month trip – we’d need to replan and pack extra supplies.” He started walking again.

Would they actually postpone the flight? Cancel it? Dad would be pissed! He had forced the decision on her; if she chose to stay on Earth, it would be forty years before she would hear him say in that sing-song voice he fell into when he was especially angry, “AN-ya, WHY did you CANCEL the FLIGHT?”

Anya said, “It’s fine. What’s the second reason not to use the deflector drive?”

Kenji tilted his head in apology. “That will have to wait, since we’re at the bridge.”

Bouncing Off the Sun

The Albatross’s bridge was clean and simple. One wall was an edge-to-edge display like the ceiling of the meeting room, the others the plain grey of foam steel, with smaller displays all over them. Despite facing to the side, the display showed the view forward from the ship, or “up” from the perspective of the room. It was unsettling at first, but Anya assumed the crew got used to it.

Even discounting the directional contradiction, there was no question the view was artificial: the anchor was a ghostly outline, with stars and the Moon behind it showing through it.

Six form-fitting chairs were spaced around the periphery of the room, suspended in gimbals that would let them take any orientation. Captain Eva Rostova was seated in the front-left chair, but stood as Anya walked in. She looked to be in her mid-forties, although that meant she could be anything from twenties to nineties anymore. Her red hair was cropped for work, not for style, and she was shorter than Anya.

Jia Li had the pilot’s chair on the front right, and she sat as if a string ran from the back of her neck to the ceiling. A stylus was tucked behind her ear, cutting through her straight black hair like a blade.

“Ms. Anya,” Eva said. “Welcome aboard. Mr. Tanaka, thank you for loaning her to us.” Kenji nodded and dropped into the right rear seat with an awkward thump, one leg hanging over the side.

“Launch plan is prepped for ten minutes from now,” Jia said precisely. Her fingers marked three quick points on the glass, not even looking at them, and a diagram sprang onto the wall display showing the Earth and the Sun, with a faint line going from the Earth to a point near the Sun, and another going from there off-screen. “We’ll do the deep well push off Earth, bounce off Sol…” She touched the controls and the display zoomed out to show the whole solar system in a traditional ‘balls on circles’ format. “… then we’ll touch up using Jupiter and Neptune and coast to 581.”

“How long will we push off the Earth?” Anya asked, no longer feigning ignorance.

Kenji chuckled, but motioned for Jia to answer. “It’s a careful dance because of Kenji’s modification, but: ten seconds, give or take,” the pilot responded. “For the first milliseconds we’ll be accelerating at well under 10^8 meters per second squared because we’re throttling to avoid pizzaing ourselves. That’ll be about 1.0 pizza-force g’s squishing us from all sides, plus 20 g’s tidal across the depth of the ship’s hull, so roughly 6 g’s pressing us into our seats; but that will only last for a couple seconds. We’ll ramp up the multiplier as we gain distance, and within a few seconds we’ll be at the full 9.2*10^11 multiplier.”

“Okay, and I’m sorry, I guess I know much more about how Armstrong walked on the Moon than about how we do it these days: we’re heading for the Sun? Why do we need to do that? Every other trip I’ve taken in the ‘Tross has been in-system and we just pushed off whatever we were near. With Kenji’s modification we’ll hit near-light-speed just pushing off the Earth for a few seconds, right?”

Jia shook her head. “Not the Sun exactly.”

Duh.

“Just a point near it. Inside the orbit of Mercury, anyway, although we’re not aiming along the orbital plane. Gliese 581 is about 10 degrees above the plane, so we’re aiming accordingly. As to why – sure, we’ll top 99% c in a few seconds pushing off Earth, but ‘near-light-speed’ isn’t measured from zero to one – it’s measured in how many ‘9’s you put after the decimal point. With Earth that’s ‘less than one,’ and at that rate the trip to 581 would take years of ship’s time.”

“Couldn’t we push off the Earth longer?”

Jia shook her head. “Not a deep enough well. If pizzafication weren’t an issue we could hit maybe one 9 and a bit, probably pack extra rations and be there in months not years. But since I don’t look good in pepperoni, we have to go easy at the start, when the acceleration is the best. So Earth won’t do. Instead, we aim for a point about 0.13 Astronomical Units – AU – from the Sun. We spend roughly fifteen minutes getting there, and then repel off the Sun, headed for 581.” She gestured and the display zoomed out again, until it showed both the Sun and Gliese 581, with a faint line between them and a small dot traveling along the line. “The Sun’s well is deep enough that we’ll easily hit 0.99999 and a bit c, and get to 581 in a few weeks ship’s time.”

“How long will we push off the Sun?”

Jia tilted her head slightly. “From our perspective? A fraction of a second. And as I said, both Jupiter and Neptune are conveniently near our path on our way out of the system. I’ll take readings as we head out, and if needed we’ll refine our trajectory using them.”

“If they weren’t there, what would we do?”

“We can catch 581 at anything up to 26 AU. As these things go, that’s a ridiculously large margin of error – your father’s ship only has a 22 million kilometer range and they’ll be fine. We’re targeting 1 AU, somewhat ecliptic north so we miss most of what 581 has flying around it. That gives us an easy path to the planet Egeria when we get there, and plenty of margin for error. With the equipment we have, we’d have to actively try to screw it up to be more than a quarter AU off-target. As it is, I’ll hit the mark within a few hundred clicks”

“Are you willing to put money on that, Pilot?” Ben asked from the entryway.

“You’re willing to bet on misfortune?” Eva said, not smiling. “That’s dark.”

Ben held up his hands. “I’m not betting on a miss, just an inaccuracy. It happens, although as you say, being able to correct with both Jupiter and Neptune means it would be a shocking failure…” he glanced significantly, but still with a smile, at Jia, “to stray even, say, 200 kilometers from dead center?”

Jia got up from her seat and walked over to Ben and slapped his face, gently but not softly. “That’s for questioning my navigational skills.” She kissed him, not gently, but softly, and continued, “And that’s for giving me your money. You’re on for twenty.”

Ben turned to Anya and said in a stage whisper, “I never expected her to take the bait at 200km.”

Anya looked around the room. Kenji was wearing a worn t-shirt featuring an anime – Lost Spirit? – Ben wore a dark blue knit sweater. Even Eva was dressed casually, in a simple striped button shirt. Only Jia wore anything remotely like a uniform, a simple black tee with a green jacket over it. She knew this was intentional, for her, the same way she knew the crew on the Albatross II would always be in uniform, and take every opportunity to say “Sir” to her father.

Anya gestured to Jia and said, “Wait, why are we targeting 1 AU away from 581? Surely that complicates the approach?”

Jia was about to respond when a light on the board that Anya had never seen as any color but green turned yellow, and an automated voice rang out throughout the ship – “Two minutes to acceleration, please prepare.”

Jia shrugged and said, “You’ll have to look it up, I gotta get to work taking Ben’s money.”

Eva sat back in her chair, but looked over her shoulder and said, “Kenji, get flat. I heard about the bet, but this is an order.” She looked at Anya while nodding at Ben, who was settling into the second chair on the right, “He bet Kenji he couldn’t stand during the initial acceleration.”

Kenji answered back, “Captain, it’s only 6 g’s.”

Calmly, Eva replied, “I want you flat and strapped in ten seconds or I’m postponing the jump and fining you for the cost to re-book the launch window.”

“Fine.” Kenji secured a belt across his waist as his chair reclined to a very-nearly-flat configuration, supporting him from head to toe.

Old School

The room was silent but for the ventilation system. Anya looked from one of them to the other, but no one said anything. All of them looked deep in thought, but she had no idea if they were thinking about a clever way to survive, or a graceful way to die. Which one was she focusing on?

“Okay, here goes.”

It wasn’t so bad; roughly what she had experienced before when panels launched, but of course that was because while she was at the edge of the ship, making the effect stronger, the panels were much farther away, making the effect weaker.

Jia filled her helmet. “Now send three more and we’ll let those coast while we wait for the first one to arrive. With the distance to the batch before, the acceleration was low – you’ll have almost twenty minutes to organize the hallway however you like.”

She sent the three, then took advantage of the time to pressurize the hallway and pack it completely full of panels, close to a hundred, now held back with straps to avoid the recoil that had injured Kenji.

When she was done she depressurized the hallway again, and if she had wanted to get to the rest of the ship she would have had to shove panels out of the airlock just to make room to get back to the interior door.

When Jia spoke again, she sounded out of breath, presumably from collecting panels inside the ship. “Okay, everyone brace for the panel. Anya, this will be full force, get clipped in. Everyone ready?”

“Yes.”

“Yes.”

“Like I said, hospital bed.”

The force this time was much stronger. Half a meter of rope slipped through the descender, and still it was jarring.

“The one is gone, and the three are properly on their way. You can send another three, and you have about six minutes to do it.”

“Why just three?” A panel disappeared into the distance.

“Ask me about four after you’ve done three a few more times.”

Another panel left. “Okay, but I’m pretty sure I can handle four.” The third panel was on its way.

Six minutes later Jia said, “Everyone brace. Ready?”

“Yes.”

“Yes.”

“Bed.”

“You’re so lazy, Kenji, do some real work.” Three strong thumps and a meter of rope. “Anya, the three are gone, and the three you just sent are on their way. You have right around two minutes.”

Feeling ornery, Anya hurried and managed to clear three panels in less than thirty seconds. To move that quickly, she overlapped the panels somewhat as they entered the field. The second panel was at a bit of an angle when it was snatched up by the drive, and the rear of it swung left as it departed and clipped the leading edge of the third panel, sending it quickly off to the right.

Fortunately Anya was guiding that panel from the rear, also to the right, so as the panel pivoted violently, the rear of it moved away from her. If she had been on the left, it would have crushed her immediately. As it was, it wrenched out of her hand, twisting her wrist. She thought she was safe, but it rebounded silently from the far side of the airlock.

She was prepared, and had the presence of mind not just to brace against the panel’s impact, but also to pull herself toward the rear of the airlock and out of the way. As the panel hit the right wall of the airlock, the front end of it entered the drive field and the entire thing rocketed forward and away. If she hadn’t gotten out of the way, the panel would have both crushed her and dragged her corpse into the drive field and out into space.

Did Eva hear that?

Eva cut through the silence. “What were those bangs?”

“One of the panels hit the other on its way out and it bounced around the airlock before taking off.”

“Okay. Be careful. Don’t die.”

“Aye aye, cap’n.” She waited. No admonishment this time.

Jia said, “In case it isn’t obvious, anything that enters that field is permanently gone from the ship, regardless of the state it’s in, or what we do after. There is zero chance of recovering it, so give the field the respect it’s due, and don’t poke even a toe outside the airlock, okay?”

“Okay.” She found herself staring out into the utter blackness again. There was zero indication they were even moving, unless you counted the blackness itself. She shuddered and tore her gaze away into the airlock.

The remainder of the two minutes ticked by, and after everyone braced, Jia said, “The previous three are gone, and those three are on their way. You can send four if you like. Two minutes.”

She sent four. Despite being more careful than before, she finished the fourth in just over thirty seconds, giving her over eighty seconds to catch her breath.

“Three away, four on their way. You want to try five?”

“Sure!”

Five took forty seconds, and she thought she could get them going a little faster to give herself a reasonable break with just a 70 second cycle time.

At first she managed it in under forty seconds, for the next batch and the one after that. She started sweating inside the suit; it tickled the small of her back as it inched down. Every seventy seconds she sent five panels out. By the eighth batch she was drenched with sweat, and her suit was warning her about how she was consuming oxygen at an accelerated rate. Two batches later, the first time it took her over a minute to clear five panels out the airlock, she said, “Okay, let’s go back to four.”

Jia, breathing hard herself from retrieving panels inside the ship, just replied, “Sounds good.”

Disruption

The hallway was mostly cleared when, during the acceleration of the panels 8200 kilometers away, the ship noticeably shuddered. Anya practically shouted in her helmet, “What happened?” as she dodged the remaining twenty or so panels, which were going in every direction against the straps that now were too loose.

There was no reply for five, ten, fifteen seconds. Then Eva’s voice rang in her ears. “Anya, do exactly as I say with no questions. Don’t bother to close the airlock doors. Get to the hatch at the end of the hallway as quickly as you can. Move! Now!”

She pulled herself through the hallway past the remaining panels, which had mostly settled down. Eva continued, “Go through the hatch.”

The air pressure won’t allow that!

She resisted interrupting with a question – barely – and Eva continued. “Get to engineering as quickly as you can. It’s depressurizing and Kenji didn’t make it to a pressure hatch fast enough.”

Well that explains it. The hatch offered no resistance – there was only vacuum on the other side, and she swarmed through.

“Get to Kenji, get him back to medical. You have less than two minutes.”

What was he doing out of medical in the first place?

She took a moment to orient herself on the far side of the hatch. There was debris everywhere, and every surface was covered with a thick white frost. Engineering was on the far side of the ship, and one level forward. The space between was mostly a lattice of girders that had once held wall panels and floors.

She pulled herself forward, careful not to go so quickly she couldn’t stop at the far end. She was focused on her target thirty meters ahead, but she couldn’t help but see that many of the wall panels they had loosened in preparation for removing them had torn free from the ship’s structure.

The door to engineering was hanging open, facing into the room within, but blocked from closing by several loose cables, tools, and a sweater. Goofy and the Pokemon were still there on the door, visible through the sublimating frost, which made Goofy’s leaking pipe look like it was forming a stalagmite.

She opened the door, careful in case Kenji was right behind it, but he was not there. She turned, and immediately saw him floating motionless by a closed hatch that led to the hallway to medical.

He didn’t just clear the door to engineering? Why? Would he even remember the reason if – no, when – they resuscitated him?

She grabbed Kenji and headed back toward medical in the center of the ship. “Depressurize the hall in front of medical,” she called out.

“Already done.”

She tried the hatch, and it opened, with minor resistance. She shoved Kenji’s still form through, went through herself, and sealed the hatch. “Okay, pressurize now!” She grabbed Kenji and pulled him into medical, setting him on the table. A robotic arm offered an oxygen mask, and she pressed it over his face and pulled the straps around the back of his head as tightly as she could. Her suit registered the pressure increasing in the room before she heard it, but before long she heard the hiss of air returning to the room. A few seconds later Jia and Eva entered, both wearing oxygen masks and portable tanks. She stepped back and they took over with Kenji. Within ninety seconds the room was at standard pressure, but they kept the oxygen mask on Kenji as they put drops in his eyes and checked him for other new injuries.

Once certain he wasn’t injured beyond the existing cracked ribs and arm, they started shaking him and massaging his face. Before long he stirred enough to look down at himself and say softly, “You couldn’t have cleaned up the piss, shit and vomit while I was asleep?” Eva smiled, and Jia almost laughed. Anya took in a deep breath, held it for a moment, and then slowly let it out.

Twenty minutes later, with Kenji still in medical having his eyes and ears tended to, the three of them met on the bridge. Once it was somewhat repressurized, the ship smelled different. The scent of the wall hangings was diminished, and a vaguely lubricant-based scent had taken its place. As Anya walked in she asked, “So what happened?”

Jia brought up a diagram of the ship on the main display. “The mass sensors out to the side aren’t nearly as good as the ones facing forward. I programmed the side deflector to skip any panels that were too close to hit one at a time reliably, but it looks like that failed. We hit about 23 g’s of compression for a moment and it seems we were right to set the limit at 12 – that was enough that we have ruptures in six locations, five around the middle of the ship, and one right near engineering that got Kenji.” The diagram lit up with multiple sections highlighted in red.

“About half the ship is depressurized. We can get that down to a third by closing the right hatches, but the rest will have to wait until later; or maybe that’s just how we arrive at 581.”

Before Jia could continue, Anya interjected, “And what was Kenji doing in engineering?”

Jia pursed her lips. “Digging out an inertial balance for when we get to the end of the panels and need to fine-tune our velocity.”

Eva raised an eyebrow and said, “We’ll have to discuss that when he’s feeling better.”

Anya managed to twitch her right eyebrow in support. Dang it!

Jia continued, “Unfortunately one of the ruptures was into the main water tank. Good news for us, much of the water flash-froze on its way out, so it’s sitting there on the outside of the ship, a big chunk of ice we’ll want to collect after we get back on course. I just checked, and we have about 87 panels left to go if we get started again in the next few minutes. 88 if we wait half an hour.”

“Look on the bright side,” Anya jumped in. “A bunch of the panels are already detached and ready to go.” She shrugged. “Let’s get started.”

Eva raised a hand. “Just a minute, please. First, no more than three panels at a time. Second, increase the spread on the initial push to make sure this doesn’t happen again. And third:” She turned to Anya and put a hand on her shoulder. “Good job getting Kenji to pressure. You absolutely saved his life; neither of us could have gotten suited up fast enough to retrieve him.”

Anya nodded. “I’m just glad I was in a position to help.”

“Okay, let’s get some panels on the way and load up the hallway.”

The distance to the previous batch was such that they had over half an hour to pack the hallway with panels, again filling it as completely as they could. Once they got the cycle straightened out they were sending three panels every two minutes.

At one point Anya happened to notice the edge of the panel she had in her hands; it was from the main entertainment room, and bore the marks of her height over the years. “One second, not this one,” she said to Jia, and set it aside to grab another.

The work went quickly. Anya was the limiting factor, even though she was sending almost ninety panels per hour. Toward the end of the hour, as the 478th panel winked out of sight, Jia announced, “We’re on track to catch 581, just barely: 2.45 million kilometers, which we will bend to 5.4 million as we decelerate. How much of a cushion do you want, Eva?”

The captain didn’t hesitate. “We don’t want to risk missing it, but the closer we get to the center, the worse the deflector risk is as we approach – so if you’re certain we’re on target, with a 10,000 kilometer buffer, we’re good.”

“I thought you’d say something like that,” Jia replied. “Anya, get out the bolts and that inertial balance, we have some fine-tuning to do.”

They ended up overshooting the mark with the 23rd bolt and had to face the drive the other way and launch screws until they were aimed 10,002 kilometers inside the target.

When they were safely on course, they unpacked the patching materials: a combination of air-tight fabric, adhesive, and fiber-reinforced foam designed to work across anything up to about a meter. After curing it with UV they pressurized each area to a tenth of an atmosphere and waited an hour to see if the patch was holding. In all cases but one it held perfectly, so they fully repressurized, using a significant portion of their reserve atmosphere, leaving the one area isolated and in vacuum, a problem for later. The ship now smelled strongly of adhesive. It wasn’t pleasant, but it was reassuring.

Eva assigned Anya to go out and chip away at the twenty-ton iceberg attached to the outside of the ship. The same logic applied as had before: with Kenji largely out of commission, Jia was too valuable to expose her to even the minor risk of a spacewalk.

Before she headed out, Jia intercepted her in the hall outside the airlock and pulled her to one side, shifting nervously. Looking anywhere but straight at her, Jia said, “I… just wanted to say to stay safe.” Her eyes went down to the left, off to the right, then resolutely focused on Anya’s. “And if something does go wrong, I’ll… I’ll be more careful this…” She froze, her eyes wide, and then she just crumpled in on herself, looking down at her feet, her shoulders shaking. She looked up again and lurched forward to grab Anya in a strong hug. Jia was wearing Ben’s sweater; it was scratchy.

What do I do, what do I do, what do I do…

“Jia, that wasn’t…” No! “You didn’t…” Also no! “I’ll be very careful.”

Jia pulled back and angled her eyes up to Anya’s. “You’d better!” She smiled quickly, and headed back for the bridge.

As it happened, the walk outside was uneventful, and after several trips eventually she recovered just over half of their water reserves.

Anya took off her helmet in the hall, having inspected the outside views of the patches even though the areas they protected were fully pressurized and holding atmosphere securely. She sealed the airlock for hopefully the last time until they reached 581. Stepping through the door at the end of the hallway, the ship was now mostly an empty grid of beams where the walls had been. Below, the storage area stood open, piled boxes of food standing beside containers of water and various replacement equipment and tools. To the left was what was left of engineering. Straight ahead she could see where Ben had examined her for the trip three… no, four days earlier. Kenji was still there, recovering. Above were the underside of the entertainment room and its adjoining rooms, where they had moved their beds and personal belongings for the remainder of the trip, and the bridge. They also had enough food and supplies that if everything else went wrong they wouldn’t need to leave that area except for medical and engineering, which would be extremely difficult to relocate.

They had put the drive engine back on gravity duty, but just at five percent: enough to hold things in place and give everything a “down” to respect, but not enough to walk comfortably, and not enough to cause a problem toward the edges of the ship – so she pulled her way up to the control room where everyone else was. She first went through two air-tight doors, then dropped her spacesuit off in what they had designated as the equipment room. When she reached the bridge Eva and Jia were in their seats; she suspected that was where they’d spend most of the remaining days of the trip. Jia was wearing Ben’s blue sweater again, as she had every day. It softened her a little.

As she pulled herself in, Jia smiled at her and said, “There’s our stoker.”

“Stoker?”

“I looked it up. It’s what they used to call the guy who maintained the fuel supply on a steamship. We’re alive because of your zero-g skills in a space suit, so thanks.”

Thank my parents – their vendettas against each other made me this way.

Anya tilted her head slightly and nodded.

Eva spoke. “We’re going to be late getting to 581. It’ll just be a couple hours —”

Anya jumped in. “Hours? We’re days late, no?”

Eva shook her head. “Relativity is tricky. We’re days late to us because of the slowdown. But regardless, the journey is going to take speed-of-light plus several hours. We would have had to drop our Lorentz down to somewhere in the forties to be days late. Still, the ship will auto-checkin as soon as we’re in proximity, and they’ll know we’re late.”

Anya shrugged. “When they hear the rest of the story the lateness won’t be the issue.”

“I imagine not,” Eva said.

“By the way Jia, I read up on the insertion procedure.”

Jia smiled in amusement. “So you understand why we don’t point straight at the star?”

“Sure – turning off the deflector is a no-go because of the incoming radiation and interstellar hydrogen, and if you’re headed right for the star, there’s no way to approach with the deflector running: you’d bounce away as soon as you were close enough to catch it.”

Jia nodded and said, “That’s the high-level, but there are more subtleties to it if you want to do the reading.”

“But presumably not everyone comes as close as we are?”

“Not generally. We had a large margin before we gave up the gravity drive, and an ordinary ship coming in with our drive wouldn’t be hitting at our speed. With a lower Lorentz they’d also be able to catch 581 at a greater distance.”

“How bad is it going to be?”

“It shouldn’t be too risky.”

“What about the radiation?”

“Radiation?”

“From 581. Since we can’t point the deflector at it, the radiance will be off the charts, right?”

“Oh — no. We can’t point the deflector at where 581 is, but we can point it at where 581 appears to be, we just have to be careful.”

“Okay, you lost me.”

“Right now 581 is almost directly in front of us. As we approach, it will move farther and farther to the side, but it won’t look that way: relativity pushes all light from anywhere ahead of us toward the point directly in front of us, and that includes the light from 581. But relativity doesn’t affect the drive that way — the drive acts on the local gravitational gradient, which shifts to the side with 581. Once we’re within about 500 AU the difference will be great enough that we won’t even need to angle the deflector; we can just point it straight ahead and miss 581. But starting in a couple weeks, and until we hit that 500 AU mark, we’ll need to angle the deflector away and be very careful not to risk hitting 581 with it. And that puts us at a very slight risk if something comes screaming in from that side. But don’t worry.”

“I won’t.” Maybe a little…

Arrival

Weeks later, Anya and Jia shifted the drive back to being a drive, over Kenji’s protests that he was mostly recovered, and they were operating in a fully weightless environment again.

With just over three hours to go, they met on the bridge for a final debrief. Anya had switched back to blonde, but a somewhat darker shade than before, and a little shorter.

Eva started. “Any update on targeting?”

Jia shook her head. “Same as the last time you asked me, we’re still dead on target.”

She turned to Kenji. “Is the drive prepared?”

“Yep. Triple checked, and then checked again this morning, just because I was bored.”

Anya jumped in, “Easy for you to say, your boredom equals work for me.” Kenji smirked at her.

Eva admonished them both with a look and continued. “The anchor is secured against the ship?”

Kenji nodded. “Yes, same answer: Anya’s quite the spacewalker, and there’s a future waiting for her in construction. The anchor and ship meet cleanly by design, but 2 g’s of tidal force is a lot to put on a metal-to-metal connection.”

Eva nodded. “And that’s why we’ll be suited up, just in case the ‘Tross springs a leak. Anything else?” She looked at each in turn, and Anya was gratified to see Eva’s grey eyes focus on her no differently than the others. “Fine. Then in just over three hours, we’ll meet here to bounce off 581. Then we’ll shed our angular velocity against Consus, and when that’s done we’ll shuttle in to Egeria in a few hours.”

Three hours later Gliese 581 was a tiny dot on the display, dead center. Anya knew that a true-color image would have been black, with all the light from 581 either blue-shifted into UV and soft x-rays without the deflector, or red-shifted into microwaves or radio waves with the deflector. The upper-left corner of the display had a column of numbers:

  • A timer to closest approach, counting down past six minutes;
  • Their Lorentz factor, sitting steady at 153;
  • The distance to 581, 109 AUs;
  • The universal acceleration, just 0.000004 g’s even though the drive was throwing everything it had at 581. Their local acceleration was a more respectable 15 g’s.

All were strapped into their seats, in their suits. Extra tanks of air were strapped in a corner, next to a container of water.

For now they were floating, weightless. As the other numbers decreased at varying rates, and the acceleration increased slowly, the Lorentz factor was stubbornly stable at 153, until, as the timer passed 90 seconds, the Lorentz number finally changed: to 152.9. Shedding relativistic speed was slow. They were at 27 AU, and their galactic deceleration had only reached 0.0006 meters per second^2, while their local acceleration was shooting past 250 g’s.

As the seconds ticked down and 581 finally started visibly growing on the display, Jia reported their location in a calm tone. “60 seconds. 20 AU. 50 seconds. 17 AU.” Several loud snaps and bangs echoed through the ship as the tidal forces began to really dig in. Finally the Lorentz number started moving as Jia counted, “40 seconds… 30… headed for closest approach and maximum tidal.” The small red circle on the display continued to grow and brighten.

Over the seconds to closest approach, tidal acceleration pressed them into their seats: not too strongly at first, but rising as their local acceleration shot up. There was a horrible metal-on-metal grinding noise as the shield settled harder on the ship. Anya imagined the ship being injured and tired, working hard to deliver them safely to their destination. I practically grew up in the Albatross, now it’s literally tearing itself apart to keep us alive.

Their galactic acceleration remained stubbornly small, but the Lorentz number finally gave way in the final seconds of approach, ticking down by tenths in a blur.

Still Jia was calm as she said “Tidal at 2.2 g’s.” The red circle grew until it filled the display. At the moment before closest approach the display was filled with red.

At the final instant, their local acceleration flashed into the millions of g’s, while the galactic finally broke into single digits. Then a split second before they passed closest approach to 581, the drive cut and they were weightless. The drive flash-rotated to point behind them to attract the star as it receded, their seats rotated to face the ship’s center, and the tidal acceleration resumed. Now they were being stretched instead of compressed, and the change was disturbing. The anchor and the ship separated with a painfully loud clang that echoed throughout the ship for several seconds.

The timer reset to 5:13 at closest approach and was counting down, now labeled “Orbit” instead of “Periapsis.” The Lorentz factor had paused for a split second at 71 at closest approach; now it was already in the 20s and dropping rapidly. The local acceleration had dropped back into the hundreds of thousands, but in the galactic frame it was climbing rapidly into the hundreds of g’s.

Jia spoke. “We’ll complete the second half of the deceleration profile as planned, stopping just shy of 1 AU. The ship didn’t give anywhere, and we appear to still be air-tight.” She checked a smaller display to the side. “We’ll end up at 7.8 degrees off the 581 ecliptic, almost exactly opposite Egeria. Consus is in a reasonable position for braking, so no special adjustments required there.”

At three minutes to go they dropped below 200,000 g’s of local acceleration, even as the galactic acceleration continued to climb past 4,500 g’s. Kenji spoke. “The drive numbers are holding up fine, although based on our earlier experience, I can’t make any predictions based on that.”

Eva looked at Kenji for a moment, then said, “No quip?”

Kenji shrugged. “Maybe I’ve matured. Or maybe I just won’t trust that we’ve made it until we’re boots-on-the-ground at Egeria.” Anya knew they still weren’t safe, but hearing Kenji say it out loud hit different.

As they approached five minutes into the deceleration Jia started counting down. “20 seconds. 7,700 g’s galactic. 10 seconds. 5… and done. Stepping down the drive, we are orbiting 581.” She exhaled.

“At 93.6% the speed of light,” Kenji added. “We’re not there yet.”

Eva focused on her display and said, “I’m sending a message to Egeria to let them know we’re here and what happened.”

Jia was focused on one of the smaller displays. She gestured a diagram onto the main display and said, “Okay, we’re a little poorly positioned to use Consus for deceleration, it’s going to take some hours to adjust. Once we’re positioned, I’ve set the profile to keep it to 8 g’s tidal compression, since the ship is half duct tape at this point. That means it will take us 196 passes to shed our angular velocity into Consus, but we’ll be orbiting once every 6 seconds at the start from our perspective, so we’ll be done in a little over half an hour. It’s going to be unpleasant.”

“Unpleasant is a funny word to use,” Kenji replied. Jia just stared at him. “More like, ‘Hold onto your butts, this is going to suck.’” He looked at Eva, who was expressionless, and said, “Maybe I’m not fully mature just yet.”

Once it started, Anya decided Kenji’s assessment was far closer to the truth than Jia’s. As they pulled into Consus’s tight orbit, over 2 g’s pulled them into their seats, but it wasn’t stable: every six seconds it went away entirely for roughly a quarter second, and then the gravity slammed back into them. The pause was accompanied by the far-off sound of the drive pivoting from 581 to Consus, applying maximum repulsion there, and then switching back to 581, along with creaks and the occasional bang of the ship trying to hold itself together. It was disturbing and painful, and it went on, minute after minute.

The main display repeatedly showed 581 to their left, and the stars wildly swinging by. Every six seconds Consus swarmed into view, looking like they would hit it, only for it to slide to the side and past them. Eva said blandly, “Jia, that display is unsettling, can you shut it off?” The display switched to a top-down view of the 581 system, with the planets enlarged and labeled, and a dot representing the ship hustling around 581 almost ten times per minute, passing by Consus each time it went around. Knowing the distances involved, that was almost worse.

It went on for over 35 minutes.

Finally, it stopped. Jia said, “We are non-relativistic. We’re safe.”

Not all of us. Anya noticed Eva staring at the empty seat second on the left, where Ben had sat, and knew she wasn’t alone in that thought.

“Can we wait to say that until we’re not all dressed in our suits riding in a ship with the hull integrity of a soap bubble?” Kenji said it like it was a joke, but it was obvious he wasn’t being humorous.

Jia nodded and continued. “Plotting an intercept course for Egeria. We’ll be there shortly.”

Eva looked up from one of the side displays. “Egeria replied. Understandably, there are a bunch of incoming messages because we’re late, even though to them it’s only a few hours.” She squinted at the display. “So many messages!” After a moment she looked up at Anya. “One in particular might interest you: your mother took a high-speed transport slightly before we left. They just completed their deceleration, and she’ll be waiting for you when we get to Egeria.”

“My mother? On a ship?” Anya took a deep breath. “Well that’s just wonderful. And my father too?”

Eva nodded. “There are messages from both of them, actually, along with their lawyers.”

“Of course.” As battered as it was, the Albatross seemed like the better option. “Any chance we can get back on course for LP 502-56, or one of those other stars farther away?” Eva snorted and shook her head. “Back to Earth? I know it sounds dramatic, but truly, one star system isn’t big enough for both my parents. Heck, one planet – Consus is just a terraforming outpost at this point, there will be nowhere to go!” She started planning ways to keep her parents at arm’s length. More construction classes — great!

“Sorry, Anya, I know this must be difficult.”

“I wish they would get over themselves and just leave me out of it. My mother hates space travel. And she definitely isn’t attached to me enough to get her all the way to Egeria. She’s screwing with my father, plain and simple.”

“You don’t know that for sure, maybe she really didn’t want to be without you for all that time.”

“Then she could have booked a flight for the two of us and we could have spent the four weeks together.”

“She only worked up the nerve at the last minute after you were gone?” Eva sounded uncertain.

Anya rolled her eyes. I think the lawyers make it clear neither of them is fighting for me out of affection. You didn’t talk with my father for more than a few minutes setting up this contract, right?

Eva shook her head uncertainly.

“I know you didn’t, because if you talked with him for even ten minutes, my mother would have come up and you’d know exactly how much animosity he holds toward her. And he doesn’t just unload on the people he hires: I hear about it, endlessly. And trust me, it’s mutual: I don’t get a break when I’m with her; if anything it’s worse. I’m not a daughter to them, I’m a bargaining chip!”

Kenji jumped in. “If you stow away on my next ship, I’ll put you to work!”

Eva remained focused on Anya as she said quietly, “Kenji, not now.” Her face softened as she continued. “That sounds terrible, and I’m sorry. With training and time you really could put some distance between you and your parents. In the near term you could definitely get a part-time gig working on Consus. We’d all be happy to recommend you to the guilds.” Jia and Kenji both nodded in agreement.

Say it – say it!

“That’s tempting, but I think I know what I want to do.”

“And what’s that?”

Anya had been staring at the deck. Now she looked up slowly. “I… want to design ships.” After the way this trip went…no, say it out loud. “After the way this trip went I have a ton of ideas.”

Eva smiled. “I can only imagine. It’s not my field, but I have a few thoughts of my own. Oh, there’s one other thing – I sent in a status report and summary of what happened as soon as we were in-system. That’ll take them some time to assess, but there’s going to be an inquiry, of course.

“They’re going to want to interview you. At length. Promoting a passenger to crew”

Crew!

“isn’t exactly standard procedure.” Eva raised one eyebrow. “Fortunately we had an unusually-capable passenger.”

Anya raised an eyebrow back – she had been practicing – and said, “I have no complaints. Thanks for the ride. Five stars, really.”
